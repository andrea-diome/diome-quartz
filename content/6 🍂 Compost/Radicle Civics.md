According to the book [Public Participation for 21st Century Democracy,](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/book/10.1002/9781119154815) the authors define participation infrastructure as: “The laws, processes, institutions, and associations that support regular opportunities for people to connect with each other, solve problems, make decisions, and celebrate community.”

For all their scaling innovation, these societal structures have largely operated under
a theory of boundaries, in and out groups, us and them.

A self-owning, autonomous civic infrastructure such as Free
House could be one of the ways to respond to the accumulation
of wealth and growing inequality as no one could acquire them as
a property; we may have temporary rights over such beings, but
they are accompanied by obligations around its care and upkeep.

It’s not simply a matter of recognising and adequately pricing
negative externalities but challenging the normalisation of
uncivic, environmentally degradational behaviours within our
economies; recognising that all externalities affect us, whether
now or later.

As societies,
we need to create robust frameworks for holding uncertainties,
recognising that many ‘truths’ may evolve and we needn’t try to
cling to them forever. We approach truth as Mikhail Bakhtin put it
– “Truth is not born nor is it to be found inside the head of an
individual person, it is born between people collectively searching
for truth, in the process of their dialogic interaction”23

Building a new theory and praxis of identity will be
fundamental to engaging and unleashing a deep praxis of
freedom.

‘Commoning’ seeks greater inclusivity, it is both an ancient
practice and a contemporary way to rethink how we can fulfil
everyday needs and ensure the thriving of all. It describes a social
practice related to production, sharing, maintaining and
distribution through the facilitation of resources and elimination
of barriers, rather than through direct control or ownership.

What if currencies and tokens (i.e. medium of exchange) were formed based on how
different actors across society create environmental and social goods, rather than on
debt? What if their formation were decentralised allowing for values to be
understood more pluralistically?

What if we were able to rethink identity and registry so civic assets/agents/
infrastructures (such as forests, rivers, schools) could be self-owning and free from
financially-driven extraction?

What if we could develop a 21st century customary ‘lawing’ which actively learns
from Indigenous and other traditional practices, was informed and shaped our laws
through our lived experiences and stories and was enforced through peer-based
mechanisms?

By
the logic of the market system, a nurse working as a paid
employee is considered value-creating (contributing to GDP),
however the same nurse working as a volunteer ‘produces no
value’.

In finance, the development of Quantamental
Analysis demonstrates the possibility of creating more
sophisticated valuation methods which rely upon the interactions
and interrelations of data within a system. Similarly, we need a
‘systemic value’ mechanism – capable of recognising
interdependency and incorporating mutual care, social labour
and the intrinsic value of nature; considering both the elements
within a system, and the system as a whole.

We should explore how agreements and laws can be
created by inclusion of all beings through ongoing sense-making
and decentralised decision-making. ‘More-than-human’ (e.g. The

Parliament of Things) and ‘machine assisted large scale sense-
making and deliberation that could help capture customs’ (e.g.

pol.is, Local Voices Network) are the 21st century aspects of
customary law.
