## Contextualization, Instantiation, Distribution

In the [previous part](https://civilizationemerging.com/new-economics-series-4/), we examined how the core pattern of extraction, abstraction, and accumulation in current economics leads to increasing fragility, inequality, and existential risk. To transition to a viable system, we need to reverse this pattern into one of contextualization, instantiation, and distribution. This part will analyze these in more depth.
### Contextualization

Contextualization means situating resources, production, value flows and economic behavior in the broadest relevant systemic contexts. This is the opposite of the reductionism inherent in extraction and abstraction, which isolates resources and value from their diverse interconnections.

Some examples of contextualization:

- Agriculture systems working with local ecology and replenishing soils, rather than maximizing extraction through monocrops, fertilizers and pesticides that damage and deplete. This recognizes the complex value of topsoil ecology and nutrient cycles.
    
- Production facilities and supply chains structured around local materials flows and cycling waste locally, rather than global chains offshoring harms. This sees production as part of regional industrial ecologies.
    
- Full cost accounting of economic activities - including impacts on community, society, ecology - not just narrowly defined financial costs. This reveals the true footprint in wider systems.
    
- Protecting the complex value of ecosystems like forests, wetlands and coral reefs for climate regulation, biodiversity, and human physical/cultural needs, rather than only seeing potential extraction value.
    

In general, contextualization means perceiving and designing systems to maximize the synergistic, emergent properties that arise from diverse agents interconnected in broader systems like societies and ecologies. It values systemic coherence. The opposite view sees resources as isolable, fungible commodities defined by narrow utility for human consumption or production.

Philosophically, extensive contextualization depends on a cognitive capacity for complex systems thinking, as well as empathetic concern about impacts on wider human and ecological communities. Current economics conditions people against both - simplifying complex systemic interdependencies down to prices and financialized cost-benefit calculations. It also frames production and consumption as an impersonal game, obscuring real needs and impacts.

However, emerging data science capabilities can help map and visualize complex interconnections computationally. And global interconnectivity enables broader human concern and responsibility across space and time. So the capability for contextualization is growing, if we cultivate the intention.

Some domains where contextualization is particularly crucial:

Agriculture: As explained above, this is humanity's original source of economic surplus, but also originally extractive and simplifying. Regenerative, symbiotic agricultural systems working with local ecologies are critical.

Technology: Exponential technologies like AI and synthetic biology could enable tremendous benefits or catastrophic risks. They must be guided by extensive contextual understanding of complex systems dynamics and ethics.

Governance: With greater interdependence, economic policy and investment must consider systemic impacts, across domains like health, ecology, culture. Not just GDP growth.

Business: Companies should account for full spectrum effects across stakeholders - workers, customers, community, environment. Not just shareholders.

Finance: As capital allocation is central, impact analysis should reflect systemic risks like inequality, political capture, climate change.

Education: Needs to develop contextual systems thinking skills and ethics, not just functional finance and economics training.

### Instantiation

Instantiation means focusing economics on directly improving real, tangible goods and quality of life. It counters the tendency towards abstracted monetary wealth in the current system. Some examples:

- Orienting policy and investment towards providing universal nutritious food, clean water, shelter, education, healthcare, etc directly.
    
- Incentivizing production of higher-quality, longer-lasting, fully recyclable goods - rather than planned obsolescent commodities that maximize consumption.
    
- Measuring value generation and impact in terms of improvements to real metrics like lifespan, knowledge, ecological regeneration, access to culture, etc. Not just monetary returns.
    
- Open collaboration and open-source approaches to ideas, IP, and knowledge creation, aiming to freely improve quality of life for all rather than extract rents.
    
- Democratic processes for determining allocation of resources towards what should be instantiated based on actual, contextualized communal needs and shared values. Not aggregate market demand abstracted from real needs.
    

Essentially, instantiation prioritizes improving the tangible, experiential fundamentals of human life and ecology, rather than increasing financial wealth as an end itself. It reconnects abstracted concepts of value back to real wellbeing. This is both a policy direction and an ethos for organizations and individuals.

Philosophically, instantiation reflects humanistic rather than instrumentalist approaches. It sees economic activity as meaningful to the degree it directly enhances life and health, not only facilitates trade of commodities.

Some domains where instantiation is particularly important:

Business: Orientation towards businesses improving real quality of life directly with their products/services - not just seeking profit.

Technology: Automating drudgery and materially improving welfare. Not just extracting data and optimizing consumption.

Finance: Funding regenerative, ethical enterprises. Not just investors maximizing portfolio returns.

Politics: Policy focused on enacting universal rights and public goods first. Not just supporting capital accumulation.

Culture: Celebrating contributions that meaningfully improve life for all, in the arts, sciences, engineering, care work etc. Not glorifying abstract financial success.

Infrastructure: Building for lifespan, aesthetics, ecology - not just economic optimization.

Medicine: Curing causes of disease and optimizing wellness. Not just treating symptoms for profit.

### Distribution

Distribution means utilizing the potential abundance enabled by technology to circulate resources in equitable, efficient flows - rather than accumulating for private gain. Some examples:

- Policies and technologies ensuring universal access to foundational goods and services - food, water, shelter, education, healthcare, transportation etc.
    
- Cooperative platforms enabling effective circulation of surplus time, space, products, skills, ideas etc where needed. Preventing unused excess in some places, scarcity in others.
    
- Shared open knowledge, IP, and digital commons, recognizing the non-rivalrous nature of information. Avoiding artificial paywalls or secrecy that stifles progress.
    
- Socio-economic systems that gather and distribute resources bottom-up through distributed sensemaking, collective intelligence, and participatory processes. Rather than top-down control.
    
- Optimization of systems to maximize usable value from finite resources via effective sharing, recycling, upcycling etc. Rather than maximizing financial accumulation or consumption throughput.
    
- Cybernetic systems balancing dynamic equilibrium of needs, rather than profit maximization and unequal accumulation. Preventing imbalances.
    

Essentially, distribution aligns with the closed-loop, cyclic networked flow dynamics of nature and ecosystems. It obsoletes the ideological basis of scarcity economics by utilizing technology's abundance potential.

Philosophically, distribution reflects collective solidarity values as opposed to individualist, competitive ones. It sees equal access as a right, not chance allocation based on private capital ownership.

Domains where distribution is critical:

Technology: AI, sensors, IoT, blockchain and other exponential technologies can enable much more effective mapping of needs and real-time coordination of resources.

Policy: Regulation of industry, IP, and markets should mandate fair, open access wherever possible. Prevent monopolies or paywalls on foundational goods.

Platforms: The majority of web services now rely on scaling free content, tools, and data created by users. But most profits go to the platforms rather than back to the value creators. Models keeping value in common should be explored.

Space: Asteroid mining and space-based solar could provide practically limitless critical resources like energy, minerals, and space. But their governance will be crucial for equitable global access.

Finance: Cryptocurrency and decentralized finance models like DAOs may help redistribute capital and value flows. But equitable, systemic distribution should be a primary design goal.

## Conclusion

This analysis explains how contextualization, instantiation, and distribution can form the kernel of a new ecological economics aligned with long-term human flourishing. It offers a constructive first-principles vision we can reference to build and evaluate economic proposals. Key next steps will be developing functioning examples of these principles across sectors like food, technology, business, governance, finance, culture and more; building out new models, metrics and institutions rooted in this paradigm; and continuing to analyze its complex intersections with the realities of the current system during the transition. With an understanding of the fundamentals, we can then begin assembling the larger puzzle towards a holistic living economy.

# New Economics Series: Part 6

## The Role of Web3 in Transitioning to New Economics

In previous parts, we examined how transitioning economics towards increased contextualization, instantiation and distribution is necessary for an optimally thriving civilization. This part provides an in-depth analysis on the potential role of web3 technologies in moving us in these directions.

### Defining Web3

The term web3 encompasses various emerging technologies seeking to build a new phase of the internet and economy based on principles of decentralization, openness and user empowerment. While definitions vary, some of its key features include:

- Decentralized protocols, organizations and services that are autonomous from national boundaries or corporate control.
    
- Cryptographic verification of identity, data provenance and digital assets enabling trustless transactions.
    
- User-owned digital assets and ventures that can be tokenized as cryptocurrency and non-fungible tokens.
    
- Direct peer-to-peer exchange of value and assets through decentralized applications and crypto wallets.
    
- Platforms, applications and algorithms governed transparently and democratically by users rather than centralized entities.
    
- An open internet of value and assets as well as information, enabling new economic and governance models.

### The Potential of Web3 for Contextualization

By expanding interoperability across data, platforms, and organizations while increasing collective intelligence, web3 architectures could enhance contextualization in economics and society in a variety of ways:

- Decentralized, permissionless, and open technology stacks can facilitate integration across fragmented data, systems, and processes. This provides a basis for understanding diverse real-world interconnections rather than only proprietary data silos.
    
- Distributed sensor networks, internet-of-things, satellite systems, and cryptographic verification of supply chain and impact data can radically increase the fidelity of system mapping. This enables closer observation and understanding of complex interdependencies in the real world.
    
- Cross-domain data pooling, predictive analytics, and causal inference on combined datasets can reveal unseen systemic relationships, tipping points, and unintended consequences. This provides the evidence base for foreseeing impacts on wider contexts.
    
- Decentralized oracles, AI assistants, multiplayer simulations, and Mixed Reality decision theaters can provide individuals and groups contextual real-time guidance for considering complex systemic interdependencies in choices.
    
- Smart contracts, DAOs, tokenized incentives, and cryptoeconomic mechanism design could encode policies and behaviors protecting broader stakeholder interests and contexts. Making contextualization operational.
    
- Planetary scale open data coordination can help manage global commons and risks requiring holistic understanding like climate, oceans, and biodiversity loss.
    

However, technology alone is insufficient for increasing contextualization. The socioeconomic frameworks, incentives, ethics, and educational systems must also evolve to encourage seeing beyond narrow self-interest. Web3 can support this shift as a platform, but not achieve it independently. Its potential must be steered towards intentional institutional and cultural change.

### The Potential of Web3 for Instantiation

By automating rote work and using tokens to incentivize tangible human activity, web3 could help redirect economics towards directly enhancing real-world welfare - instantiating real improvements versus chasing financial abstractions:

- Embedding internet-of-things, sensors, and AI in the physical world can enable automation of drudgery and material optimization of infrastructure, manufacturing, agriculture, transportation, energy and material access. Advancing abundance.
    
- But benefits will require stewarding the development of exponential technologies responsibly. Cryptographic verification, transparency, and ethical oversight mechanisms built into web3 systems could help ensure AI and biotech promote flourishing.
    
- Platforms utilizing cryptographic identity, portable reputation, and multi-dimensional credentialing could instantiate richer social and cultural realities by incentivizing development of knowledge, character, relationships, reputation - not just financial wealth.
    
- Blockchains of on-chain real-world assets and activities could tokenize building infrastructure, cleaning environments, mentoring youth, creating beneficial digital commons and other social goods - rewarding those generating tangible instantiations of value.
    
- Cryptographic trustless transactions can guarantee incomes, welfare, human rights and access to social goods directly - without relying on corruptible centralized authorities.
    
- DAOs and smart contracts could encode shared values and fund protection of public commons, knowledge, and regulation of natural monopolies. Instantiating the shared infrastructure for common prosperity.
    
- But purely technical systems will not lead to instantiation of human potential without supporting cultural evolution - we must design web3 to elevate the human spirit beyond materialism.
    

The risk remains that web3 could be co-opted towards further financialization, information exploitation, and consumerism without conscious guidance. Its use must be integrated into more holistic economic transition efforts.

### The Potential of Web3 for Distribution

By enabling transparency, decentralized organization, and boundaryless value flows, web3 could support equitable distribution and circular economics:

- Cryptographically verified ledger systems like blockchain can remove opacity and corruption from financial, production and distribution networks - mapping real resource flows across the planet.
    
- Cryptocurrencies allow direct, permissionless, censorship-resistant exchange of value peer-to-peer across borders - potentially decreasing reliance on exploitative institutions.
    
- Smart contracts could automate conditional aid disbursement, credit provisioning, and reparative resource transfers according to transparent rules.
    
- Cryptoeconomics and token engineering can design incentive patterns for efficient, bottom-up distribution of data, assets, resources to where they are most effectively utilized - as conditions change.
    
- Non-fungible tokens enable decentralized shared governance and benefit sharing of digital commons like knowledge, IP, arts, software, protocols, rather than only private accumulation.
    
- Distributed asset registries can track real world objects for sharing, recycling, upcycling to maximize their utilization and circular flow.
    
- But philosophical and policy shifts are still required for economic justice - technology alone will not transform inequality and institutional path dependencies.
    

As with other aspects, web3's greatest potential impact on equitable distribution may be from accelerating cultural shifts, through exposing hypocrisies, inspiring visions, and efficiency pressures. This requires ongoing guidance.

## Conclusion

In summary, while still in its infancy, web3 represents a significant new platform layer and toolset for transitioning towards an economic system aligned with holistic context, instantiated human development, and equitable distribution. However, as emphasized throughout, pursuing this potential requires proactive, thoughtful leadership to develop web3 within an integrated socioeconomic transition strategy. Technology alone will not lead to systems evolution. Domains like enterprise, governance, culture, education, policy must proactively co-evolve alongside web3 infrastructure for a holistic paradigm shift. With wisdom, web3 may help accelerate an economics of thriving. Without it, web3 risks compounding existential dangers. Our collective response must rise to this challenge.
