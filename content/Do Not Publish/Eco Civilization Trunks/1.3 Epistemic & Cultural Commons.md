## What is it?
Epistemic and cultural commons refers to the accumulation, application and dissemination of knowledge and wisdom. It is not just about having access to information but also about the ability to use that information in meaningful ways. At the core, it contains the shared values, beliefs, and practices that define how we understand and act on the world.

As technology develops, new ways of learning, thinking and transmitting information emerge, while many ancient mediums maintain their relevancy. As we continue to understand the world, tell stories and shaping our media, it's important to ask how this epistemology is shaping us.

## What does it look like now?
As social and environmental issues compounds, we're starting to understand that the root of the problem lies in thinking of ourselves as separate from nature. This [mechanistic (or reductionist) paradigm](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/311674632_Technology_and_Ecology) originates in the European scientific revolution, and views nature as a machine without life or spirit, made up of discrete components governed by mechanical forces. Nature and all other living creatures are objects to be used by humans, the subjects, for material gain. Therefore only we can view the world ‘objectively’, because we are separate from it.

> _From ancient times the main goal of science has been gaining wisdom and understanding while remaining in harmony with nature. However in the Western world, since Bacon, the goal of science has tended to be patriarchal and has largely involved the pursuit of knowledge in order to control and exploit nature._ David Grierson, [Technology and Ecology](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/311674632_Technology_and_Ecology)

While this reductionist view improved living standards in Europe, human bodies were being reduced to numbers in a ledger, their personal data tracked by the people who claimed to own them. At the same time as reductionist knowledge was being developed at high speed, the complex systems knowledge of indigenous communities was being erased, dismissed as "unscientific."

> *The most important domination was the mental universe of the colonized, the control, through culture, of how people perceived themselves and their relationship to the world. Economic and political control can never be complete or effective without mental control. To control a people’s culture is to control their tools of self-definition in relationship to others.

As this colonial system gave way to capitalism, aligning society around morality and values was also deemed insufficiently 'objective.' Neoliberalism proposed that in fact the market was a way to create knowledge, because it quantified all of these subjective experiences and exposed them numerically through prices. Today, it is not just the market that is shaped by our experiences, it is the market that shapes our beliefs, knowledge and behaviors. We can see many symptoms of this mechanisation and commercialization of knowledge:

- The industrial model of education, with its emphasis on conformity, discipline, and standardized testing, mirroring a factory that can stifle creativity, critical thinking, and the natural curiosity vital for genuine learning.
- An average 10- to 14-year-old [cannot identify 10 native plants](https://georgiawildlife.com/out-my-backdoor-connect-kids-nearby-nature) growing nearby but can recognize 1,000 corporate logos.
- The prevalence of social media and the dominance of short-form content have contributed to a culture of shallow engagement, where the depth and nuance of complex ideas are lost.
- The influence of private capital on knowledge creation, as seen in sectors like pharmaceuticals, can skew research priorities and access to knowledge, prioritizing profit over public good.
- Artificial intelligence, built on the vast commons of shared data and knowledge (like that from Wikipedia), and the hard work of often low-waged workers, is centralizing and enclosing this knowledge for private gain.
- As more value is placed on productivity over the intrinsic worth of individuals, there's little room for the reflective practices and spaces necessary for making sense of the world.
- As data is often equated with knowledge, the deeper, more nuanced aspects of wisdom—gained through experience, reflection, and ethical consideration—are undervalued. This preference for quantifiable data over qualitative insights diminishes the richness of human understanding.
- Storytelling and cultural expressions are primarily used as marketing tools, undermining its potential to convey complex cultural narratives and values.
