## What is it?
Institutions are structures of social order and cooperation governing the behavior of a set of individuals within a given human community. They can be formal, such as legal systems, governments, and corporations, or informal, like traditions, social norms, and cultural practices.

Governance refers to the processes, systems, mechanisms and relationships through which decisions are made within an institution. For example, a government (institution) can use a referendum (governance process) for deciding whether or not to implement a specific policy. The question of who can participate in governance often depends on ownership (who controls the resources or institutions) and identity (who is represented or has agency within these institutions).

> _The philosopher **Magnus Vinding**, suggests in his new book, **Reasoned Politics**, that “politics” has two layers: a values layer (establishing what we want and why) and an empirical layer (establishing what is true about getting where we want, so as to inform policy and action). I think that works well: governance, in a comprehensive sense, is about coordinating actions, including establishing the values from which we govern and agree to be being governed._ [Hanzi Freinacht](https://medium.com/@hanzifreinacht/3-design-principles-for-protopian-governance-bc2bfa7faa9a)

## What does it look like now?
Formal institutions in modern-day society are usually divided into 3 categories:
- **Government:** characterized by their authority to levy taxes, enforce laws, and regulate activities within their jurisdiction. In most modern democracies, governments are selected through party-driven elections and operate under a mandate to serve the public interest.
- **Non-profit sector:** characterized by their focus on mission-driven objectives rather than profit. They often rely on volunteer work, donations, grants, or in some cases revenue-generating activities to support their mission.
- **Business:** play a crucial role in delivering essential goods and services. Characterized by their focus on profit-making for owners and shareholders. Leadership and decision-making processes are defined internally for that purpose.

In short, these institutions are differentiated by what they are allowed to do, who they serve and how they generate income. However, just as with [Prevailing Economic Systems](https://diome.xyz/4+%F0%9F%8C%B0+Seeds/Prevailing+Economic+Systems), when we scratch the surface, the lines between them blur, and we have to ask whether these were even the right lines to draw in the first place.

In many countries, it is codified in law that governments decisions should reflect the will of the people and business decisions should benefit their shareholders. But when even the decisions of how to make a frying pan can [poison our drinking water](https://www.nytimes.com/2023/08/16/magazine/pfas-toxic-chemicals.html) for generations or how to regulate social media content can [spark a genocide](https://www.amnesty.org.uk/press-releases/myanmar-meta-must-pay-reparations-rohingya-people-role-ethnic-cleansing), this distinction makes less sense.

On the other hand, [representative democracies](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=k8vVEbCquMw&ab_channel=%23WHYMAPS) inadvertently breed corruption and partisanship. Because elections select for electability and not for good governance, they become contests of popularity and financial clout rather than forums for genuine debate, leading to policies that prioritize powerful interests and short-term gains over long-term public welfare (a dynamic that is being magnified by [Social Media](https://diome.xyz/4+%F0%9F%8C%B0+Seeds/Social+Media)). By casting a vote, you are forced to put complete trust in a representative, with very few ways to hold them accountable to their promises.

Issues that span multiple jurisdictions, such as water governance and migrant crises, highlight the limitations of traditional governance structures in managing transboundary challenges. At the same time, businesses controlled by the few operate freely across national boundaries, choosing the jurisdictions with the most beneficial arrangements for profit extraction.

We are operating in an increasingly [complex environment](https://thecynefin.co/about-us/about-cynefin-framework/), but the dominant approach to decision-making is based on [mechanistic and reductionist thinking](https://demos.co.uk/wp-content/uploads/files/systemfailure2.pdf) (a dynamic that is being magnified by the use of [Data, Algorithms & AI](https://diome.xyz/2+%F0%9F%8C%BF+Leaves/Data%2C+Algorithms+%26+AI)). This is leading to the marketization of everything from civic life to ecosystem dynamics in the name of continued growth and increased efficiency (as seen in [Criticism of Market Societies](https://diome.xyz/2+%F0%9F%8C%BF+Leaves/Criticism+of+Market+Societies)). We need to rethink our governance systems & institutions to serve life in all of its complexity, based on both human [Value & Values](https://diome.xyz/4+%F0%9F%8C%B0+Seeds/Value+%26+Values).

## What solution hypotheses do we have?
### Collective Intelligence
Collective intelligence refers to the capacity of groups, communities, or entire societies to pool knowledge, wisdom, and expertise in order to make decisions, solve problems, and govern more effectively. It leverages diversity of thought, interdisciplinary approaches, and participatory mechanisms to enhance the quality and inclusiveness of governance.

> _Fewer distortions of information and suppression of voices and interests tends to create more intelligent and tenable decisions... To increase collective intelligence often means to move complex decisions into the realm of participatory and deliberative democracies — i.e. involving stakeholders and creating citizen councils selected by sortition (lottery) to discuss a matter before a policy is decided upon. In a way, this means that fewer people get a say which might seem “less democratic”—but at the same time, a richer and more nuanced picture can come to the fore, and this in turn means that people’s opinions on a matter can less easily be manipulated by smaller elites or interest groups._ [Hanzi Freinacht](https://medium.com/@hanzifreinacht/3-design-principles-for-protopian-governance-bc2bfa7faa9a)

### Distributed Power Structures
Distributed power structures follow 3 key principles: Ownership, Subsidiarity, Interdependence, Diversity & Emergence. (1) Ownership means all affected stakeholders have access to meaningful participation. (2) Subsidiarity means following a holonic structure, where decision-making groups are nested from the small & local to the global scale, and decisions are made at the lowest possible level. (3) Interdependence means these groups form a meshwork, erasing artificial separations between groups, organizations or geographies. (4) Diversity means not just involving multiple voices, but allowing for [multiple choices, opportunities, and encounters](https://provocations.darkmatterlabs.org/re-permissioning-the-city-unlocking-cities-growing-underutilised-spatial-assets-for-an-emergent-1550997714a4). (5) Emergence is like "birds in a flock that, over time, [builds coherence](https://provocations.darkmatterlabs.org/re-permissioning-the-city-unlocking-cities-growing-underutilised-spatial-assets-for-an-emergent-1550997714a4) and order by simply responding to the movements of their neighbours."
