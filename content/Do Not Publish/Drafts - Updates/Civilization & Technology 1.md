## What is a civilization?
> *Civilization  is  an  info-(energetic-)material  structure  developed  by  humans  to effectively cope  with  themselves,  nature,  and  their  perceived  creator.* [A Grand Model of Civilization](https://scholarsarchive.byu.edu/ccr/vol51/iss51/7/)

A civilization is a complex socio-technological system developed by humans to organize life and meet their collective needs. It arises from the interplay between a group's worldviews, social institutions, and technological infrastructures. Civilizations are not static entities but dynamic, adaptive systems that continually respond to internal and external forces. 

![[CM Civilization.png]]

Civilizations are deeply influenced by and, in turn, influence human physiology, interspecies relationships, and planetary ecosystems. For example, the characteristics of the ecosystem where a civilization develops (planetary structure) influences its agricultural practices (infrastructure), which will in turn shape its governance system (social structure) and religious beliefs (superstructure). Disruptions in the planetary structure, such as climate change or the depletion of essential resources, can put pressure on human societies to adapt their technologies, economies, and lifestyles, leading to cascading effects across all other elements.

The civilizational frameworks presented above are based on the work of a number of scholars including Marx, Feliks Koneczny's Quincunx, Targowski's [Tri Element Model](https://scholarsarchive.byu.edu/ccr/vol51/iss51/7/), Marvin Harris in [[Cultural Materialism]], Ken Wilber's Integral Theory, and The Emerenge Projects' [integration](https://eonv.org/design-criteria-2/) of the two previous frameworks.
### What is [[1.3.1 Superstructure]]?
The superstructure encompasses a society's beliefs, values, and worldviews, shaping and being shaped by how its members perceive and interact with the world. It includes the symbolic and ideational aspects of a culture, such as:
- **Religion**: Belief systems, rituals, and religious institutions that provide meaning and guidance for human existence.
- **Art and Literature**: Cultural expressions and artistic practices that reflect and shape a society's values and narratives.
- **Ideology**: Overarching worldviews, values, and belief systems that justify and support the existing social order.

The way our perception of ourselves is formed, as well as how we interpret information, is mediated by superstructure. Rather than "seeing is believing," our beliefs fundamentally influence what we perceive.
### What is [[1.3.2 Social Structure]]?
Social structure refers to the organized set of institutions, norms, and relationships that shape and regulate the behavior and interactions of individuals within a society. It includes:
- **Domestic Economy**: Family organization and household economic activities.
- **Political Economy**: Patterns of authority, laws, and governance within a society, including the organization of power and the distribution of resources.

Many of our interactions with each other are mediated by social structure. For example, the family unit acts as a core social structure, governing dynamics like gender roles, distribution of labor, and norms around intimate relationships.
### What is [[1.3.3 Infrastructure]]?
Infrastructure refers to the integrated ecologies of technologies that provision society's essential needs, including:
- **Means of Production**: Physical and technological means necessary for producing goods and services (e.g., factories, machinery, tools).
- **Technological Innovations**: Advancements that affect productivity, efficiency, and the capacity to harness and utilize natural resources.

Most of our interactions with the world are mediated by infrastructure. You might be sitting in your house right now. In that case, your house is a technological infrastructure by which you're currently interacting with the planetary ecosystem.
## Technology
### What is technology?
**Technology is the act of structuring of materials, energy, information to adapt & thrive.** It comprises the tools, techniques, and systems that humans create to meet our needs and shape our interactions with the environment.

Infrastructure is composed of [ecologies of technologies](https://consilienceproject.org/technology-is-not-values-neutral)—interrelated sets of technologies that co-evolve symbiotically. For example, personal computing involves the microchip, hard drive, screen, mouse, modem, broadband networks, and server infrastructure working together as an integrated system.

However, technology extends beyond just infrastructure. Social structures can also be conceptualized as forms of social technology. Institutions, such as governments and corporations, along with encoded rules like laws, serve as prime examples of social technologies. These entities structure and codify information into patterns of behavior and interaction, enabling organized and predictable societal functions.

Psychotechnologies—techniques and practices designed to influence mental processes, emotional states, and behavioral patterns—directly impact the superstructure of society by shaping individual consciousness. Examples of psychotechnologies include meditation practices, psychotherapy techniques, and religious or cultural rituals.
### MEIN model of technology
At a fundamental level, technologies can be understood as operating through the dynamic interplay of four key elements:

- **Materials**: The building blocks that are transformed through various processes to create functional artifacts.
- **Energy**: The fundamental driver that powers physical processes, material transformations, and information flows.
- **Information**: The organizing blueprints, communication systems, and control mechanisms for coordination.
- **Networks**: The structural frameworks that facilitate interactions between energy, materials, and information flows.

We'll call this the [[MEI Model]] of technology in civilization.

Biological systems illustrate how networks, energy, materials, and information integrate to create conditions for life. The human body relies on interconnected networks - cardiovascular for circulating oxygen and nutrients, nervous for processing signals and maintaining homeostasis. Energy from food fuels metabolic processes, while the brain coordinates information flows to regulate systems like respiration and digestion.

Technological systems operate as an analogous *societal physiology*. Networks like transportation, power grids, and telecommunications form structural pathways akin to circulatory, nervous, and digestive systems. Energy powers economic production, enabling the extraction and transformation of materials. Information systems like markets and institutions provide regulatory mechanisms to coordinate these networks. In this way, technological infrastructures, guided by information flows, harness available energy and materials to provision societies with goods and services.
### Technology & Influence
> *Technologies always create the potential for new forms of behavior, values, and thought, even when the technology is not explicitly made to do so.* [Technology is Not Values Neutral: Ending the Reign of Nihilistic Design](https://consilienceproject.org/technology-is-not-values-neutral)

Technologies are shaped by the interplay of societal superstructures, social structures, and infrastructures. For example, the development of the printing press was influenced by the Renaissance's emphasis on knowledge-sharing (superstructure), enabled by the the rise of affluent merchant classes and relatively stable centralized governments (social structure), while building upon prior innovations like movable type and the printing mechanism itself (infrastructure).

However, technologies are not merely passive products of civilization - they actively reconfigure the very contexts that gave rise to them. Technologies reshape how we perceive and interact with the world by introducing new constraints and affordances that alter the boundaries of time, space, relationships, and values. 

- **Time**: Technologies affect our perceptions of what is [likely to be true in the future](https://stark.mirror.xyz/n2UpRqwdf7yjuiPKVICPpGoUNeDhlWxGqjulrlpyYi0), as well as what was true in the past.
- **Space**: Technologies can create and transcend spatial constraints, reshaping the experience and politics of geography, distance, and place.
- **Relationships**: Technologies mediate the types and qualities of relationships possible between agents.
- **Values**: Technologies open up new modes of being that cultivate revisioned value systems.

In this way, technologies are imbued with the priorities and belief systems of their creators, which then propagate through societies in complex, often unpredictable ways. This is particularly true of information technologies, which directly shape how we process, share, and construct meaning. 
## Civilizational Systems
### Metasystems in Civilization
Civilizational metasystems emerge when structural changes exert pressures that demand greater information processing capabilities from a society. The emergence of new technologies for sharing and processing information - like the development of language, writing, or the printing press - enables qualitatively new forms of interaction between human groups. These novel interaction potentials then pave the way for higher levels of societal integration and more complex organizational structures to take shape.
### Historical Civilizational Systems
Human civilizations have generally stabilized within three broad levels of complex organization, each coevolving with distinct energy sources, materials, information mediums, social structures, and superstructures:

While this advancement is sometimes portrayed as universally positive social progress, such transitions have often come at significant costs and disruptions for the humans involved. For instance, while foraging societies have been described as "the original affluent society," the shift to agrarian lifestyles frequently entailed more arduous and monotonous labor. Similarly, the industrialization process violently displaced countless individuals from their homes and livelihoods through enclosures and forced urbanization.

1. **Foraging Societies:**
    - **Energy Source:** Hunting and gathering.
    - **Materials:** Natural resources directly available from the environment.
    - **Information Medium:** Ritualized Language.
    - **Societal Structure:** Small, egalitarian bands and tribes.
    - **Superstructure:** Animism and nature-based spiritual beliefs. Societies viewed themselves as part of the natural world, with a deep respect for the environment and its resources.
2. **Agrarian Societies:**
    - **Energy Source:** Agriculture (domesticated plants and animals).
    - **Materials:** Cultivated land, domesticated animals.
    - **Information Medium:** Writing.
    - **Societal Structure:** Chiefdoms and kingdoms with hierarchical structures.
    - **Superstructure:** Poly- and monotheism upholding the divine right of rulers. Belief systems often centered around agricultural deities and the sanctity of the king or chieftain as a mediator between the gods and the people.
3. **Industrial Societies:**
    - **Energy Source:** Fossil fuels (coal, oil, natural gas).
    - **Materials:** Industrial raw materials (metals, minerals).
    - **Information Medium:** Printing press and telecommunications.
    - **Societal Structure:** Nation-states with centralized governance.
    - **Superstructure:** Rationalism and nationalism. Enlightenment ideals emphasized scientific progress, individual rights, and national identity. Growth became a dominant ideology.
### Civilizational Transition
It's important to note that more research is still needed to fully understand the nuanced dynamics driving these civilizational upheavals. For example, one theory posits that the pivotal transition from foraging to agrarian societies was primarily catalyzed by the extraction of wealth through conquest and subjugation (financial extraction), rather than being solely propelled by the invention of writing systems. A more holistic understanding of these processes is crucial as we navigate the pressures and opportunities of our current civilizational crossroads.
### Civilizational Failure
Civilizations have collapsed catastrophically many times throughout history, and there are recurring patterns that have contributed to their demise. These underlying drivers of civilizational failure are now intensifying, posing unprecedented risks in our modern era.
#### Exponential technology
Exponential technologies are those capable of self-replication or compounding growth, allowing them to multiply their impacts – for better or worse – at an accelerating rate. Agriculture was the original exponential technology, enabling human populations to expand rapidly while disconnecting us from being an integrated part of natural ecosystems. 

The development of nuclear weapons marked a pivotal moment when humanity acquired an exponential technology with the potential to trigger global catastrophe. Today, emerging technologies like artificial intelligence, biotechnology, and advanced manufacturing techniques are democratizing the ability to wield world-altering forces of creation and destruction. Unless we establish new frameworks for responsibly harnessing such exponential technologies, we risk irreparable damage to our world.
#### Externalities
Externalities are the unaccounted-for consequences of our actions. They arise from a linear, reductionist mindset that fails to account for the intricate web of interconnections and feedback loops operating across social, economic, and environmental domains.

Our current civilizational model is predicated on the perpetual growth of consumption and extraction, generating a torrent of externalities that are undermining the very ecological foundations of human prosperity. From climate change and biodiversity loss to toxic pollution and the degradation of natural resources, externalities are rapidly destabilizing the Earth's regulatory systems.

Moreover, our technological systems are producing pernicious social and psychological externalities, from the erosion of privacy and autonomy to the fragmentation of public discourse and the proliferation of mental health issues exacerbated by social media and digital addictions.

As these externalities compound and propagate through complex systemic feedbacks, they are accelerating the degradation of our ecological and social capital – the integrated totality of life-sustaining resources and socioeconomic resilience upon which all civilizations ultimately depend.

![[Ecocultural Capital.png]]
#### Multi-polar traps
Multi-polar traps are interconnected dilemmas where the rational choices for individual actors directly undermine the collective good. These traps arise when the pursuit of narrow self-interest generates negative externalities that ultimately harm all parties involved.

Classic examples include the tragedy of the commons, where individuals deplete shared resources for personal gain, and regulatory races-to-the-bottom, where jurisdictions compete to attract business by lowering environmental and labor standards. In our globalized world, multi-polar traps are becoming increasingly pervasive and severe. Nations prioritize economic competitiveness over environmental sustainability, while corporations off-shore pollution and exploit regulatory havens.

As the interconnectedness and complexity of our civilizational systems intensify, these multi-polar traps are becoming more acute and intractable, frustrating our ability to address collective challenges like climate change, resource depletion, and economic inequality.
### Transition Pathways
Humanity now faces three broad potential trajectories or "attractors" as we grapple with these civilizational crises:

1. **Techno-Authoritarianism**: Where technological acceleration centralizes power and resources in the hands of a small elite, leading to extreme inequality, social unrest, and authoritarian control.
2. **Civilizational Collapse**: Where compounding crises and cascading systems failures trigger a descent into chaos, conflict, and the unraveling of complex human societies.
3. **[[1.3 Infrastructure]]**: Where humanity transcends its current dysfunctional paradigms through the cooperative creation of new distributed and decentralized models for organizing technology, energy, and resources to cultivate a thriving future for all.

Realizing the emancipatory potential of a [[1.3 Infrastructure]] will require nothing less than a profound civilizational transition. This transition is already underway, catalyzed by the democratizing forces of internet technologies and the imperative to harness new, renewable forms of energy to power a new format of human civilization.

Yet the path ahead remains uncertain, fraught with obstacles and requiring unprecedented levels of cooperation, foresight, and innovation across all sectors of human endeavor. 

Only by reconstructing our systems from first principles can we navigate the turbulent transitions ahead and give birth to an anti-fragile, [[1.3 Infrastructure]] capable of flourishing through these challenger.
