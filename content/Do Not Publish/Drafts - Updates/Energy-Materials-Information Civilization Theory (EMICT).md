## Information-Energy Metasystem Model
The article by Cadell Last discusses the potential for a transformative shift in human governance through the development of a new [[Information-energy metasystem model]](IEMM). 

**Key Points of the IEMM:**
1. **Human Evolutionary Stages and Governance:**
   - Throughout history, human governance has evolved through distinct stages characterized by the use of specific information and energy mediums:
     - Bands/tribes stabilized by language and hunting.
     - Chiefdoms/kingdoms stabilized by writing and agriculture.
     - Nation-states stabilized by the printing press and industrial energy sources.

2. **Current Challenges:**
   - Modern nation-states are increasingly inadequate in addressing global problems like climate change, economic inequality, and technological advancements.
   - The emergence of the internet and renewable energy sources provides a new context for potential governance structures that can operate beyond the limitations of nation-states.

3. **The Global Brain Hypothesis:**
   - Suggests a future where the internet facilitates a planetary intelligence, leading to more distributed and self-organizing forms of control, as opposed to centralized hierarchical structures.

4. **Information and Energy in Control Systems:**
   - The IEMM emphasizes the importance of integrating new information mediums (e.g., the internet) with sustainable energy systems (e.g., solar power) to create stable and effective control systems.
   - The evolution of control systems relies on the meaningful use of information and energy, focusing on the relational capacity to utilize these resources for cybernetic processes.

5. **Future Prospects:**
   - The IEMM predicts a potential transition to a new metasystem where global governance is more distributed, democratic, and based on renewable energy sources.
   - This new system would prioritize equitable distribution of resources and decentralized decision-making, harnessing the collective intelligence of humanity.

6. **Distributed E-Democracy:**
   - Proposes a framework for global governance through digital, distributed, and democratic mechanisms, leveraging the internet for large-scale argumentation and decision-making.
   - Emphasizes the importance of a participatory democracy where people vote on ideas and policies directly, rather than through representatives.

7. **Implications for Policy and Society:**
   - The transition to a new metasystem requires substantial innovation in control structures and a cultural shift towards more egalitarian and inclusive governance models.
   - Focus on fostering mechanisms for trust, reputation, and motivation within digital participatory platforms to ensure effective governance.

### Theoretical Foundations of IEMM: MSTT and CIT
The Information-Energy Metasystem Model (IEMM) integrates two core theoretical frameworks to contextualize human governance transitions and propose a future trajectory for societal organization: Metasystem Transition Theory (MSTT) and Control Information Theory (CIT).
#### Metasystem Transition Theory (MSTT)
**Overview:**
MSTT, proposed by Turchin (1977), explains how higher levels of system organization emerge from the coordination of previously independent subsystems. This theory is used to understand evolutionary leaps in complexity, both in biological systems and human societies.

**Key Concepts:**
- **Metasystem:** A higher-order system that controls and integrates multiple subsystems.
- **Transition:** The process through which these higher-order systems emerge, often marked by a qualitative shift in organization and complexity.
- **Step Function:** Represents the transition as a sudden leap to a new level of complexity, visualized as a sigmoid (S-shaped) curve where the integration of subsystems accelerates before stabilizing at a new level.

**Application to Human History:**
- **Information Mediums:** MSTT identifies critical periods in human history where new information mediums (language, writing, printing press) enabled the transition to more complex societal structures (bands/tribes, chiefdoms/kingdoms, nation-states).
- **Current Context:** The internet is seen as the latest information medium that could enable the next metasystem transition, potentially integrating nation-states into a global or "glocal" governance system.

#### Control Information Theory (CIT)
**Overview:**
CIT, developed by Corning (2007), focuses on the role of information in controlling energy utilization within living systems. It extends traditional information theory by incorporating the functional and meaningful aspects of information used for cybernetic processes (control and feedback).

**Key Concepts:**
- **Control Information (IC):** The capacity to use information to acquire, control, and utilize energy for purposeful activities. It emphasizes the relational aspect of information and energy in achieving control.
- **Information Potentiality (IP):** Increased access to information enhances potential control capabilities but requires meaningful organization to be effective.
- **IC Formula:** \( IC = \frac{AU}{Ai} \)
  - **AU (Available Utility):** Total quantity of energy accessible for control processes.
  - **Ai (Available Information):** Total energy cost associated with bringing AU under control and utilizing it.

**Application to Human Evolution:**
- **Energy Sources:** Human societies have evolved by stabilizing around primary energy sources (hunting, agriculture, industrialization) and corresponding information mediums (language, writing, printing press).
- **Current Context:** The next metasystem could stabilize around renewable energy sources and the internet as an information medium, creating a more sustainable and distributed control system.
- **Functional Synergies:** Successful governance models will require integrating new information processing capabilities to create synergies that enhance control and feedback mechanisms.
- **Metasystem Stability:** Stability depends on effective IC feedback. Breakdowns in IC can lead to disintegration, while synergies can enable higher-level integration.
### Energy Sources & Modes of Production
In the context of Cadell Last's Information-Energy Metasystem Model (IEMM), the term "energy source" is used to broadly encompass both the primary means of energy acquisition and the dominant mode of production that sustains human societies at different stages of their evolution. Here’s a breakdown of how these terms are used and what they signify:
#### Historical Energy Sources as Modes of Production
1. **Hunting (Foraging Societies):**
   - **Energy Source:** Wild plants and animals.
   - **Mode of Production:** Hunting and gathering.
   - **Information Medium:** Language.
   - **Societal Organization:** Bands and tribes.
   - **Description:** Early human societies relied on the direct acquisition of food through hunting and gathering. Language facilitated communication and coordination in these small, relatively egalitarian groups.

2. **Agriculture (Agrarian Societies):**
   - **Energy Source:** Domesticated plants and animals.
   - **Mode of Production:** Farming and animal husbandry.
   - **Information Medium:** Writing.
   - **Societal Organization:** Chiefdoms and kingdoms.
   - **Description:** The advent of agriculture allowed humans to produce surplus food, leading to larger, more settled populations. Writing enabled record-keeping, administration, and the development of complex hierarchies.

3. **Industrial (Industrial Societies):**
   - **Energy Source:** Fossil fuels (coal, oil, natural gas).
   - **Mode of Production:** Industrial manufacturing and mechanization.
   - **Information Medium:** Printing press.
   - **Societal Organization:** Nation-states.
   - **Description:** The industrial revolution marked a significant increase in production capacity and efficiency through mechanization powered by fossil fuels. The printing press enabled mass communication, contributing to the spread of knowledge and the rise of modern nation-states.
### Future Energy Source: Renewable Energy
4. **Renewable Energy (Potential Future Societies):**
   - **Energy Source:** Solar power, along with other renewable sources (e.g., wind, geothermal).
   - **Mode of Production:** Sustainable energy generation and utilization.
   - **Information Medium:** The internet.
   - **Societal Organization:** Distributed, glocal (global-local) governance systems.
   - **Description:** Looking forward, the IEMM predicts a shift towards renewable energy sources, with solar power being a prominent example. This transition is expected to be accompanied by the widespread use of the internet for information dissemination and coordination, potentially leading to more decentralized and democratic forms of governance.

## The Thermodynamics of Degrowth
In an [episode from the "Planet Critical" podcast](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=M01Q3ZR-Mzs&t=4532s&ab_channel=Planet%3ACritical), Tim Garrett discusses the complex relationship between energy consumption, material transformation, and economic growth, applying the principles of thermodynamics to understand the challenges facing human civilization. Key points from the conversation include:

1. **Energy and Civilization:**
   - Garrett explains how human civilization has transformed the Earth's crust into infrastructure, contributing to global warming through the release of carbon dioxide. This transformation is driven by our energy consumption, primarily from fossil fuels.

2. **Thermodynamics and Growth:**
   - The behavior of economies and civilizations can be understood through the laws of thermodynamics, similar to how snowflakes and clouds form and behave. Growth in economies requires a surplus of energy, which is used not just for sustenance but also for expansion and innovation.

3. **Renewable Energy:**
   - Garrett is skeptical that renewable energy sources can fully replace fossil fuels. He argues that while renewable energy may help reduce emissions, it might also enable further growth and increased energy consumption, continuing the cycle of environmental degradation.

4. **Inequality and Energy:**
   - There is a direct relationship between energy consumption and inequality. High-energy societies tend to have greater inequality. Garrett suggests that addressing inequality requires reducing overall energy consumption.

5. **Degrowth:**
   - Degrowth involves reducing energy and material demands to create a more equitable and sustainable society. This would require a significant cultural and political shift, moving away from the current growth-centric paradigm.

6. **Collapse vs. Decline:**
   - Garrett discusses the possibility of societal collapse if current energy consumption trends continue. He contrasts this with a managed decline, where societies intentionally reduce energy use and material consumption to stabilize and potentially recover.

7. **Physics and Policy:**
   - There is a need to integrate the principles of physics into economic and policy decisions to address the intertwined crises of energy, economy, and environment. Garrett criticizes the disconnect between economists and scientists, urging for more interdisciplinary collaboration to find solutions.

8. **Future Prospects:**
   - The conversation highlights the importance of rethinking our approach to energy, economy, and growth. While the challenges are immense, there is potential for creating a more resilient and equitable world if societies can adapt to new ways of organizing and consuming resources.

### On Networks
Tim Garrett emphasizes the critical role of networks in understanding the dynamics of energy consumption, economic production, and societal growth. Here are the key points he makes about networks:

1. **Networks as Fundamental Structures:**
   - Garrett explains that all aspects of civilization can be seen as interconnected networks. These networks include physical infrastructures like roads, electrical grids, and telecommunications systems, as well as biological networks such as the cardiovascular and nervous systems in humans.

2. **Energy Flow through Networks:**
   - Energy is dissipated through these networks. The ability of a society to harness energy and convert it into useful work is largely dependent on the efficiency and extent of its networks. For example, the transportation of goods, the flow of information, and the delivery of services all rely on well-functioning networks.

3. **Material Transformation via Networks:**
   - The transformation of raw materials into the infrastructure and artifacts of civilization is facilitated by energy flowing through networks. This process includes everything from building roads and cities to producing and transporting food.

4. **Growth and Network Expansion:**
   - Economic growth is tied to the expansion and intensification of networks. As societies grow, they develop more extensive and complex networks, which enable higher energy consumption and greater material transformation. This, in turn, supports further growth in a feedback loop.

5. **Inequality within Networks:**
   - High-energy societies tend to exhibit significant inequality. This is because the distribution of resources and benefits within networks often follows power laws, where a few nodes (individuals or entities) control a large portion of the resources, while many others control very little.

6. **Maintenance and Decay of Networks:**
   - Networks require continuous energy input for maintenance and growth. Without sufficient energy, networks begin to decay. This decay leads to a loss of functionality and efficiency, which can contribute to societal collapse.

7. **Renewable Energy and Network Dynamics:**
   - The transition to renewable energy sources impacts how networks are maintained and expanded. While renewable energy can reduce environmental impacts, it may also facilitate further growth and complexity of networks, potentially exacerbating inequality and resource consumption if not managed properly.

## Energy-Materials-Information Civilization Theory (EMICT)
networks as the structural framework that enables the interaction between energy, materials, and information.
### Core Principles
1. **Energy as the Driver:**
   - **Role:** Energy is the fundamental driver of all physical and biological processes in civilization.
   - **Historical Impact:** The primary energy sources (hunting, agriculture, fossil fuels) have historically shaped the modes of production, societal structures, and network complexity.
   - **Growth Dependency:** Surplus energy is critical for growth. Without an energy surplus, societies cannot expand or innovate.

2. **Materials as the Medium:**
   - **Transformation:** Materials, derived from the Earth's crust, are transformed into the infrastructure and artifacts of civilization. This transformation is facilitated by the availability of energy.
   - **Societal Development:** The exploitation and transformation of materials are integral to the development and maintenance of complex societies and their networks.
   - **Network Foundation:** Physical networks (infrastructure, supply chains) are constructed from these materials, enabling energy flow and societal growth.

3. **Information as the Organizer:**
   - **Blueprint:** Information provides the blueprint for organizing and managing energy and materials. It encompasses knowledge, technologies, and communication systems that enable coordination and control.
   - **Historical Transitions:** New information mediums (language, writing, printing press, internet) have driven major transitions in societal organization and the complexity of networks.
   - **Network Management:** Information networks facilitate the management and optimization of energy and material flows within societal structures.

4. **Networks as the Infra-Structure:**
   - **Interconnected Systems:** Networks (physical, biological, digital) are the structural framework that facilitates the interaction between energy, materials, and information.
   - **Energy Flow:** Energy is dissipated through these networks, driving societal functions and growth.
   - **Material Transformation:** The transformation of raw materials into usable forms relies on efficient network systems.
   - **Information Distribution:** Information networks ensure the dissemination and accessibility of knowledge, enabling coordination and control.

5. **Superstructure as the Guiding Framework:**
   - **Belief Systems:** The superstructure consists of the belief systems, ideologies, and worldviews that provide meaning, cohesion, and direction to societies.
   - **Influence:** These belief systems influence how societies perceive and interact with their energy sources, materials, and information mediums.
   - **Social Structures:** Social structures and hierarchies are shaped by the dominant superstructure, which guides the development and functioning of networks within the society.

6. **Social Structures as the Framework:**
   - **Hierarchy and Organization:** Social structures define the hierarchy and organization within a society, influencing the distribution of resources and power.
   - **Adaptation and Resilience:** The adaptability and resilience of social structures determine how effectively a society can respond to changes in energy availability, material resources, and information technology.
   - **Network Interplay:** Social structures interact with and shape the development of networks, impacting the flow of energy, materials, and information.

### Historical Transitions
1. **Foraging Societies:**
    - **Energy Source:** Hunting and gathering.
    - **Materials:** Natural resources directly available from the environment.
    - **Information Medium:** Ritualized Language.
    - **Societal Structure:** Small, egalitarian bands and tribes.
    - **Superstructure:** Animism and nature-based spiritual beliefs. Societies viewed themselves as part of the natural world, with a deep respect for the environment and its resources.
2. **Agrarian Societies:**
    - **Energy Source:** Agriculture (domesticated plants and animals).
    - **Materials:** Cultivated land, domesticated animals.
    - **Information Medium:** Writing.
    - **Societal Structure:** Chiefdoms and kingdoms with hierarchical structures.
    - **Superstructure:** Polytheism and the divine right of rulers. Belief systems often centered around agricultural deities and the sanctity of the king or chieftain as a mediator between the gods and the people.
3. **Industrial Societies:**
    - **Energy Source:** Fossil fuels (coal, oil, natural gas).
    - **Materials:** Industrial raw materials (metals, minerals).
    - **Information Medium:** Printing press and telecommunications.
    - **Societal Structure:** Nation-states with centralized governance.
    - **Superstructure:** Rationalism and nationalism. Enlightenment ideals emphasized scientific progress, individual rights, and national identity. Capitalism and industrial growth became dominant ideologies.
4. **Information Societies:**
    - **Energy Source:** Emerging renewables (solar, wind, geothermal).
    - **Materials:** Advanced materials (electronics, composites).
    - **Information Medium:** Internet and digital technologies.
    - **Societal Structure:** Potential for distributed, decentralized governance ("glocal" systems).
    - **Superstructure:** Digital pluralism and global interconnectedness. Emerging worldviews emphasize interconnectedness, sustainability, and digital democracy. There is a growing awareness of global citizenship and the need for collaborative solutions to planetary challenges.
### Integrated Model of Transition
**From Industrial to Information Society:**
   - **Energy Transition:**
     - **Decentralization:** Shift from centralized fossil fuel power plants to decentralized renewable energy sources. Implement smart grids to efficiently manage energy distribution.
     - **Innovation:** Invest in energy storage technologies and grid infrastructure to support intermittent renewable energy sources.
     - **Policy:** Encourage policies that promote renewable energy adoption and phase out subsidies for fossil fuels.
   - **Material Transition:**
     - **Circular Economy:** Develop networks for recycling and sustainable material use. Promote the design of products for longevity, repairability, and recyclability.
     - **Local Production:** Utilize technologies like 3D printing to decentralize manufacturing, reducing the need for long supply chains.
     - **Resource Management:** Implement policies and technologies for efficient resource extraction and minimal environmental impact.
   - **Information Transition:**
     - **Digital Infrastructure:** Expand high-speed internet access globally, ensuring that all regions can participate in the digital economy.
     - **Data Management:** Develop robust systems for managing data privacy, security, and ethical use of information.
     - **Collaboration Platforms:** Foster the development of platforms that enable collaborative knowledge creation and problem-solving.
   - **Superstructure Transition:**
     - **Education and Awareness:** Promote education that emphasizes ecological literacy, digital skills, and global citizenship.
     - **Cultural Shift:** Encourage cultural narratives that value regeneration, cooperation, and long-term thinking.
   - **Social Structure Transition:**
     - **Institutional Change:** Support the evolution of governance structures to be more participatory and adaptive, leveraging digital tools for greater transparency and inclusiveness.
     - **Participatory Governance:** Foster participatory governance models that engage citizens in decision-making processes, leveraging digital technologies for broader involvement.
