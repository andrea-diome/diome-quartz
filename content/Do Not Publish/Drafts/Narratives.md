## What are narratives?
> *According to narrative theory (Freeman, 1993; Murray, 1999; Sarbin, 1986), a narrative is an “organized interpretation of a sequence of events.” It includes “attributing agency to the characters in the narrative and inferring causal links between events.”* [Interpreting Our Reality Through Story](https://www.psychologytoday.com/gb/blog/the-power-personal-narrative/201810/interpreting-our-reality-through-story)

Narratives are organized pieces of communication that follow a sequence - like chapters in a book or scenes in a movie. When we tell or listen to a narrative, we're not just passing along facts, but engaging in a meaningful exchange. Narratives are the way we make sense of our experiences.

> *"A wider definition of narrative would include anything which tells a story: paintings, pictures, sacred objects and the modern age blogs and other URL related material, for example references to social computing sites such as YouTube or Flickr."* [Narrative research](https://narrate.typepad.com/100816-narrative-research_snowden-final.pdf)

Narratives are an information technology (see: [[MEI Model]]) that shapes how we see the world and interact with each other. If "Artificial Intelligence" can be said to be made of algorithms, human intelligence is [made of narratives](https://theconvivialsociety.substack.com/p/narrative-collapse).
## Narrative Thinking
> *"Our considerable capacity to utilise and blend patterns is the basis of our intelligence, and evolutionary adaptability is more associated with pattern utilisation than information processing."* [Narrative research](https://narrate.typepad.com/100816-narrative-research_snowden-final.pdf)

Human cognition operates in at least two distinct modes. The first is information processing - the systematic, analytical thinking we associate with traditional science and mathematics. This approach involves working with formal systems, establishing empirical truths, and following structured procedures to reach verifiable conclusions. Think of how a computer processes data or how a scientist follows the scientific method. 

While we often associate intelligence to this logical and analytical mode of thinking, research suggests that our true cognitive superpower lies in our ability to recognize, utilize, and blend patterns. [Pattern processing](https://narrate.typepad.com/100816-narrative-research_snowden-final.pdf) is deeply rooted in our lived experiences, cultural background, and the stories we tell. It's how we navigate complex situations without consciously analyzing every detail. Like a master chess player who "just knows" the right move or an experienced doctor who can diagnose a condition at a glance, we unconsciously recognize patterns that help us make sense of the world.

> *"Our ability to link and blend patterns in unusual ways, known as conceptual blending (Fauconnier & Turner 2002), gives us ability to adapt rapidly to changing context and critically to innovate as well as to use that most powerful tool of explanation, knowledge transfer and teaching, **metaphor**."* [Narrative research](https://narrate.typepad.com/100816-narrative-research_snowden-final.pdf)

Traditional decision-making models assumed humans operate like computers, methodically analyzing all available data before reaching conclusions. Reality tells a different story. Research shows that we typically [scan only 5-10%](https://narrate.typepad.com/100816-narrative-research_snowden-final.pdf) of available information, matching it against patterns stored in our long-term memory. 

It's a remarkably efficient strategy that has evolved over millennia. Narratives serve as sophisticated knowledge-mapping tools, creating rich contexts that help us extract decisions, make judgments, and access knowledge.

> *The original use of narrative was a source for mapping knowledge... it arose from the deeply practical need to create a rich context from which it was possible to extract decisions and judgments to ask questions about knowledge in use (Snowden 1999). Narrative here was also shown to be a better recall mechanism for hidden knowledge than questions.* [Narrative research](https://narrate.typepad.com/100816-narrative-research_snowden-final.pdf)

This pattern-based, narrative approach to decision-making is particularly evident in high-stakes situations. Gary Klein's research in Naturalistic Decision Making (NDM) reveals how experienced professionals make critical decisions. They:
- Recognize patterns from past experiences
- Construct stories to understand the current situation
- Mentally simulate potential actions and their consequences
- Act based on the first workable solution they identify

A powerful example from Klein's research illustrates this process: A firefighter commander responding to what seemed like a routine house fire sensed something was wrong based on subtle, almost unconscious pattern recognition. The fire wasn't behaving quite right. Acting on this intuition, he ordered his team to evacuate. Moments later, the floor collapsed - revealing that the fire had been burning in the basement, not the ground floor as it appeared. The commander couldn't immediately explain his decision, but his pattern-recognition abilities, honed through years of experience, had helped him construct a story about what might be happening - a story that ultimately saved lives.

This natural, pattern-based approach to understanding and decision-making lies at the heart of human cognition. It's how we innovate, adapt, and make sense of our complex world. While formal analytical thinking certainly has its place, it's our ability to recognize and blend patterns that truly defines human intelligence.
## Collective Cultural Narratives
> *"A major distinguishing feature of human intelligence has been our ability to manipulate our environment, to create cultures that increase familial and tribal bonds and to pass on knowledge other than through genetic evolution and experience: we are in our very essence storytellers."* [Narrative research](https://narrate.typepad.com/100816-narrative-research_snowden-final.pdf)

The [patterns we use](https://narrate.typepad.com/100816-narrative-research_snowden-final.pdf) to understand the world and make decisions come from various sources:
1. Our genes, which give us certain predispositions
2. Our experiences, especially when we've learned from mistakes
3. Our unique ability to share knowledge through storytelling and culture

Most animals can learn from genes or personal experience. Humans have a unique ability to create cultures that pass on knowledge through stories. This ability to share information through language and culture has been crucial to our evolution. Cultural narratives serve as sophisticated knowledge management systems that embed multiple layers of knowledge within memorable, transmissible forms. 

Indigenous Australian [songlines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Songline), for example, not only describe routes through the land through song, serving as a kind of musical map. They also encode vast ammounts of cultural information including proper social behavior, historical events, spiritual significance of places, and much more. In North America, the Haudenosaunee taught their children about advanced farming techniques, important calendar events and community values through [the story of the Corn Husk Woman](https://cocreationstudio.mit.edu/decentralized-storytelling/), represented through cornhusk dolls.

Storytelling traditions are still [alive and well](https://thecynefin.co/alignment-through-action-narrative/). The firefighter commander mentioned previously developed his expertise through narrative - while technical details can be written in manuals, the more subtle aspects of tacit knowledge and critical judgment come from stories shared between practitioners: "I had a fire like this once..." These narratives transmit crucial knowledge about subtle patterns, unexpected complications, and handling uncertainty and pressure.

Collective narratives serve as sophisticated pattern libraries that support sensemaking, decision-making and action. They are how we construct our shared social reality. These shared stories shape our beliefs, values, and behaviors, helping us make sense of our world and our place in it. Our shared narratives define what patterns we are able to recognize, how we interpret those patterns, and how we build stories about the future.

> *“'Man is in his actions and practice, as well as in his fictions,' MacIntyre argued... 'I can only answer the question What am I to do? if I can answer the prior question Of what story or stories do I find myself a part?'"* [Narrative Collapse](https://theconvivialsociety.substack.com/p/narrative-collapse)

The relationship between individual and collective narratives [can be understood as fractal](https://thecynefin.co/fractal-narratives-identity/?srsltid=AfmBOoqYcNxxc5Is5AHrr0KEwqih_vrgneObG0_NZiU7sOU094V_XpLf), which means it exhibits self-similarity across different scales. The structure and patterns of stories repeat themselves at various levels of society, from the individual to the collective. At the individual level, we have personal stories that shape our identity and worldview. As we zoom out, we see family narratives, then community narratives, organizational narratives, national narratives, and so on. Each level contains stories that are distinct yet interconnected, forming a complex web of narratives.

> *"In a network of stories, each thread is a symbiotic relationship. Between storyteller and audience, & between ourselves and each story."* [Storytelling as Gift, Storytelling as Currency](https://www.are.na/editorial/storytelling-as-gift-storytelling-as-currency)

Micro-interactions at the human level feed into the collective narratives, while these in turn shape individual behaviour... As noted in research, "Group behaviour can be understood as attempts to [change the world](https://compass.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1111/spc3.12670) according to a specific collectively shared narrative about social reality."

> *"It is through the internalisation of these narratives that individuals can make sense of their lives and what happens in society; collective narratives are used to interpret socio-political events and other aspects of social reality through the lens of values and beliefs contained in these narratives. For example, COVID-19 vaccine hesitancy is closely related to beliefs reflecting a lack of recognition of the collective importance of reducing the spread of the virus, and beliefs about the vaccine being neither safe nor effective (Aw et al., [2021](https://compass.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1111/spc3.12670#spc312670-bib-0002))."* [From cooperation to conflict](https://compass.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1111/spc3.12670)

Access to and identification with certain narratives defines group membership, while sharing stories strengthens social bonds. Whether in religious communities interpreting creation stories, political movements sharing historical interpretations, or professional groups exchanging expertise, these narratives create what philosopher Byung-Chul Han calls a ["closed order"](https://www.frontporchrepublic.com/2024/05/rendering-me-into-we-a-review-of-the-crisis-of-narration/) - a framework that provides meaning and orientation in life.

## Narratives, Value and Economy
> *"Economies form in collective spaces when people define what they value as a group. Economies are an indication that a community has not only defined values but also the means of interacting with one another via exchange."* [What is Decentralized Storytelling?](https://cocreationstudio.mit.edu/decentralized-storytelling/)

Narratives are the invisible infrastructure of our economic world. From wapum to modern stock markets, human exchange has always been powered by shared narratives that tell us what's valuable and why. (see: [[Value & Values]])

> *"One of our main economic instruments was wampum, which many people incorrectly believed to be money... Wampum was a contract, but it was inseparable from the oratory structure of negotiating contracts, which was also meant to protect and communicate the shared values of the community. It was a record of an agreement that had been reached through the process of storytelling as communication. Wampum is a process of encoding abstract agreements — stories are literally woven into the material."* [What is Decentralized Storytelling?](https://cocreationstudio.mit.edu/decentralized-storytelling/)

This same principle operates in modern markets. When we buy Tesla stock, we're not just purchasing a share of a car company – we're buying into a story about the future of transportation ("electric vehicles are the future"). The value doesn't come from the stock certificate itself, just as wampum's value wasn't in the beads, but in what the community collectively agrees they represent. As more people  engage with this value by buying Tesla stock, their actions put more Teslas on the road, strengthening the narrative.

These narrative-driven economies don't just apply to individual organizations, but to entire ideological movements. This is what Nadia Asparouhova calls [idea machines](https://nadia.xyz/idea-machines) – self-sustaining systems that turn shared beliefs into real-world outcomes. An idea machine starts with a compelling story, gathers believers, develops a plan, and attracts resources. Each element reinforces the others: the story attracts the community, the community refines the story, funders validate the story with resources, and successful outcomes make the story more compelling.

![[Pasted image 20241028161716.png]]

Stories in communities aren't simply traded like commodities. They're gifted, like heirlooms, creating ongoing relationships rather than closed transactions. When experienced members share key [narratives with newcomers](https://thecynefin.co/alignment-through-action-narrative/), they're not just transferring information – they're extending an open-ended invitation to belong, to participate in shaping the story's future.

> *"I would prefer to think of telling a story as 'an open-ended contract, just like a promise or a gift,' rather than an unrippling transaction or commodity."* [Storytelling as Gift, Storytelling as Currency](https://www.are.na/editorial/storytelling-as-gift-storytelling-as-currency)

This is why narrative economics isn't just about believing in stories – it's about belonging to them. Whether we're trading beads, shares, or ideas, we're really exchanging pieces of a larger narrative that binds us together and drives us forward. In this light, every economic exchange is a story being told, and every story shared is an economy in motion.

## Narrative Collapse
In an age of unprecedented connectivity and information sharing, our traditional ways of making meaning through narrative are under strain. 

The challenge we face today emerges from several intersecting forces that are reshaping our relationship with narrative. Where modernism once offered grand, unified stories about human progress and destiny, and postmodernism worked to deconstruct these narratives, we now find ourselves in need of rebuilding useful narratives that can guide us without falling into the traps of oversimplification or dogmatism.

%%Maybe its a crisis of narratives (as differentiated from meaning?) or that's at least part of the problem - metamodernism%%

The crisis we face isn't about the absence of narratives - we have more stories than ever before. Rather, it's about our changing relationship with these stories and their diminishing ability to create genuine community, provide meaningful orientation, and help us make sense of our world. As we move forward, the challenge will be to find ways to preserve and create narratives that can survive these pressures while still serving their essential human functions.

### Homogeneity
A more recent threat comes from the homogenizing effect of modern technology. As noted in "After Software Eats the World," artificial intelligence and large language models tend to be "centripetal rather than centrifugal" - they pull toward the center of culture rather than its creative edges. This technological compression of narrative diversity threatens to flatten the rich variety of human storytelling into a more uniform, predictable form.

> *"This has important implications, when combined with Gopnik’s thesis that large models are increasingly important engines of cultural reproduction. Such models will probably not subject human culture to the curse of recursion, in which noise feeds upon noise. Instead, they will parse human culture with a lossiness that skews, so that central aspects of that culture are accentuated, and sparser aspects disappear in translation."* [After software eats the world, what comes out the other end?](https://www.programmablemutter.com/p/after-software-eats-the-world-what)

### Marketization
"We were storytellers; we have become storysellers," observes philosopher Byung-Chul Han. In our digital age, stories are increasingly commodified - transformed into content for consumption rather than vehicles for meaning and community building. 

> *"'We deploy heart-rate data from Fitbits to tell yawnsome just-so stories about fitness journeys; we embellish the tale of what we did on our holidays with selfies and soft-porn snaps of the meal we had at that cute bar we found, according to the permissible parameters of human leisure time, in Oslo. Something has gone missing in all these stories: our individuality, our humanity, our ability to tell convincing narratives rather than perform ourselves."* [The Crisis of Narration by Byung-Chul Han review – how big tech altered the narrative](https://www.theguardian.com/books/2024/feb/18/the-crisis-of-narration-byung-chul-han-review)

> *"Narratives can create real community where 'storyselling' creates only ephemeral communities organized around perpetual consumption, what he elsewhere terms 'swarms...' When certain goods are embellished with moral narratives such as ‘fair trade’, even morality becomes consumable. The moral narrative becomes information, and is sold and consumed as a product’s distinguishing feature. Moral consumption, mediated by narratives, increases only our own sense of self-worth. Through these narratives, we refer not to a community that is to be improved, but only to our own egos.”* [Rendering Me into We: A Review of The Crisis of Narration](https://www.frontporchrepublic.com/2024/05/rendering-me-into-we-a-review-of-the-crisis-of-narration/)

### Database
>*“When you read a narrative, for example, you are encountering the product of a series of choices that have already been made for you by the author out of a myriad of possibilities from the database of language. The countless other choices that were possible are present only to the imagination. You see the words the author chose, not the ones she could’ve chosen. You see the path marked out for you as a reader, not the multiple paths that were rejected. When you encounter a database, however, you see the opposite. You see the field of possibility and any number of paths through the database remain hypothetical and potential...*
>
>*It’s not that we are literally presented with a relational database [online], but we are presented with what amounts to a loosely arranged set of data points whose significance and meaning has not been baked into the form itself. Moreover, I can make my way online with a high degree of independence relative to how I might make my way through an analog media artifact. Finally, consider how digital media transforms even the traditional media artifact itself into a kind of database. A movie, for example, is transformed into a series of snippets, clips, lines, gifs, etc., which float around independently of the whole. The digitized artifact is the artifact that has, for all intents and purposes, surrendered its integrity...*
>
>*I’m not suggesting we no longer encounter narratives. On the contrary, narratives proliferate in the digital world. Rather, I’m suggesting that the database experience frames the encounter with narratives so that we experience narratives as just so many entries in a database of information. The narratives that we encounter thus fail to be comprehensive accounts of our experience or broadly compelling."* [Narrative Collapse](https://theconvivialsociety.substack.com/p/narrative-collapse)


## Narrative Regeneration
> *"If humans are pattern processors, then understanding people will involve the management of those patterns, both stimulating relevant ones to the forefront of the long term memory, disrupting established patterns to create the preconditions for innovation, and increasing the number of patterns available and their contextual relevance for decision makers."* [Narrative research](https://narrate.typepad.com/100816-narrative-research_snowden-final.pdf)

In order to understand and influence people, we need to manage these patterns. This might mean bringing certain patterns to mind, breaking old patterns to encourage new ideas, or helping people develop more diverse and relevant patterns for decision-making.

At the heart of this project is a view of meta-narrative as an emergent property or strange attractor arising from social interaction which is discoverable and actionable in the sense of quantum mechanics rather than the laws of motion. By taking narrative as a fragmented form of support for cognition, and using the ubiquity of the web and social computing, together with the representational and information processing capacity of computers, we can considerably augment and enhance the natural pattern based intelligence that underpins human decision making, and more so to radically reduce interpretative conflict in the process. [Narrative research](https://narrate.typepad.com/100816-narrative-research_snowden-final.pdf)

### Transculturalism
*"**Transculturalism** — the view we shall be exploring here, and which I hold can and should be a part of an effective **Gemeinschaft** Politics. The transculturalist position holds that it is both true that diversity is good, that racism, inequality, and discrimination are real issues with their own respective (often postcolonial) historical roots, **and** that there are real problems of integration and inter-cultural relations, as well as real limitations to and problems inherent in the cultures of different ethnic groups and cultures in society. As such, it takes a transformational view on ethnic groups (whether these happen to be constructed along the lines of race, nationality, ethnic denominations, or religious practices) and holds them responsible vis-à-vis one another as parts of “the whole” of society that results from their interactions. This sometimes involves making a value judgment of or comparison between the ethical desirability of cultures."* [The Failure of Multiculturalism and Its Resolution: Transculturalism](https://metamoderna.org/the-failure-of-multiculturalism-and-its-resolution-transculturalism/)
