**Why Do We Need Regenerative Information Technology?**

In today's rapidly evolving digital age, the need for responsible and sustainable technology solutions has become paramount. But as our world faces pressing issues such as climate change, simply incorporating green practices isn't enough. Enter the concept of regenerative information technology—a holistic approach that recognizes the profound interconnectedness of our information systems, societal structures, and the natural world. This article delves into the need for regenerative IT and how it can shape our future.

---

**1. What is Regenerative Information Technology?**

Information Technology (IT) encompasses more than just computers and telecommunication systems. In its broadest sense, IT is a tapestry woven from various threads of human culture, governance, economic systems, and so on. Historically, tools like writing, storytelling, music, laws, and even money have all been modes of information technology, instrumental in shaping human societies and behaviors. This expansive perspective, termed "Psychotechnology" by John Vervaeke, underlines that IT doesn't merely refer to electronic gadgets but to systems and tools that shape our cognition, values, and behaviors.

Regenerative Information Technology, thus, is a paradigm that goes beyond the conventional. It seeks not just to sustain but to actively restore and nurture. At its heart, it's about using tools, systems, and structures that actively promote the health of our planet, society, and individual psyche.

---

**2. Do We Really Need More Technology to Address Climate Change?**

The blunt answer? Yes. But the nuanced explanation reveals that the kind of technology we need may be different from what most imagine. It's not merely about constructing colossal machines to suck carbon from the atmosphere or bioengineering crops to withstand harsh climates.

The dire predicaments we face—rising global temperatures, erratic weather patterns, loss of biodiversity—are symptoms of a deeper issue. At its core, the climate crisis is a manifestation of a failure in coordination. Our societal, economic, and political structures have become so intricate, so deeply entrenched in their ways, that they resist positive change, even when survival calls for it.

Historical theories shed light on how societies crumble due to their own complexity and decreasing adaptability. Our modern world, while technologically advanced, grapples with the same challenge. The real culprits lie in our information or social technologies, which include our cultural, political, and economic structures.

Therefore, the technology we need isn't just physical machinery. We need a comprehensive revamp of our societal systems, our communication channels, and our decision-making processes.

---

**3. The Dual Tipping Points: Biome and Society**

While much emphasis has been given to environmental tipping points—the thresholds beyond which our natural systems might undergo irreversible change—there's less discourse on societal tipping points. Just as there are vital signs for ecosystems, there exist indicators for societies. Anomalies in mental health rates, political unrest, economic disparities, and more hint at deeper structural issues. Addressing climate change isn't solely an environmental endeavor—it's intricately tied with reshaping our societal structures.

---

**4. Biophillic & Biomimetic Systems: The Way Forward**

The regenerative model emphasizes two pivotal concepts: Biophilia and Biomimicry.

**Biophillic** systems are those that encourage a profound love for life. They emphasize well-being, resilience, and harmony, creating conditions where life in all its diversity can thrive.

**Biomimetic** systems, on the other hand, draw inspiration from nature. They emulate the intricate patterns, evolutionary strategies, and circular processes observed in natural ecosystems.

Incorporating these principles into our information technologies means adopting systems that evolve organically, emphasize circularity, and prioritize the interconnectedness of life.

---

**5. The Dawn of a Regenerative Era**

We stand at a crossroads. One path leads to further deterioration, both ecologically and socially. The other beckons towards regeneration, restoration, and resilience. The tools to navigate this choice lie in redefining our relationship with technology. Not as mere gadgets and gizmos, but as holistic systems that shape society, influence behaviors, and, if channeled rightly, can heal our world.

Regenerative Information Technology isn't a lofty ideal; it's an urgent imperative. As we reimagine our technological landscape, we're not just saving the planet; we're reclaiming our humanity.

**Why Do We Need Regenerative Information Technology? A Scholarly Exploration: Including Philosophy, Cognitive Extensions, and Sustainability**

_Abstract:_ As we navigate the complexities of the digital era and ecological uncertainty, understanding the implications of Regenerative Information Technology (RIT) becomes paramount. This paper delves deeper into the interplay between philosophy, cognitive extension through software, and their influence on sustainability.

---

**1. Introduction: Broadening the Definition of Information Technology**

Vervaeke's exploration of "Psychotechnology" expands the definition of IT to encompass a myriad of human-made tools—storytelling, music, laws—that have significantly shaped human behavior and societal structures [1].

---

**2. Technology as Philosophy in Practice: The Essence of Social Engineering**

Heidegger, in his seminal work, "The Question Concerning Technology," argues that technology is an embodiment of human thinking and values; it's more than just tools—it's a way of understanding and engaging with the world [2]. Building technology becomes an act of implementing philosophy; it transforms societal norms, behaviors, and overall human experience. As Winner puts it, "technologies are structures of order that human beings establish in their world" [3].

---

**3. Cognitive Extensions: From Language to Software**

The concept of "extended mind" introduced by Clark and Chalmers posits that external entities can become part of a cognitive process, thereby suggesting that tools like language, math, or software extend our mental capacities [4]. Haraway's "Cyborg Manifesto" further underscores that we are already cyborgs in a world where machines and humans are intricately intertwined [5]. The software, much like language or math, can influence and extend our cognitive faculties. This implies that software development isn't just a technological endeavor; it's a deeply philosophical and cognitive one.

---

**4. Software, Sustainability, and Societal Behavior**

If software extends and shapes our cognitive processes, it subsequently affects our behaviors both individually and collectively. Lanier, in "You Are Not a Gadget," accentuates how software design can influence societal norms and values [6]. Thus, regenerative software that promotes sustainability could arguably shift societal behaviors towards more ecologically harmonious actions.

---

**5. The Intersection of Regenerative Thought and Technology**

Drawing from principles of Biophillic & Biomimetic systems [7][8], embedding them in our software design could lead to technologies that prioritize the interconnectedness of life. Given the deep influence of software on cognition and behavior, RIT could be pivotal in steering society towards a sustainable future.

---

**6. Conclusions: The Interwoven Fabric of Technology, Cognition, and Regeneration**

The philosophical underpinnings of technology, combined with our cognitive relationship with software, foreground the urgency of Regenerative Information Technology. As technology becomes an extension of our very cognition, RIT presents an opportunity to weave sustainability into the very fabric of societal thought and behavior.
