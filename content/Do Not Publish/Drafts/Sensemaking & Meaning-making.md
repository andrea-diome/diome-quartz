## Sensemaking
> *"A process through which we turn information (symbols and data) we perceive in the world into (shared) understanding and meaning. Sensemaking connects experiential insights and more formal evidence sources (e.g., intelligence). It generates learning, and in turn, enables us to take action."* [Making sense of all the sense making](https://thomasmtaston.medium.com/making-sense-of-all-the-sense-making-c6d6eff2994f)

>*"Sensemaking involves turning circumstances into a situation that is comprehended explicitly in words and that serves as a springboard into action... Sensemaking is central because it is the primary site where meanings materialize that inform and constrain identity and action."* [Organizing and the Process of Sensemaking](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/211395920_Organizing_and_the_Process_of_Sensemaking)

> *"At its highest level of technique, it is a specialised function that human social systems perform when negotiating the differences inherent to themselves & of the contexts they operate in.*" [8 Design Principles for generative Sensemaking](https://static1.squarespace.com/static/6358fcee9747a0469c1398a6/t/65856807306a57677fea8579/1703241737324/8+Principles+of+Sensemaking.pdf)

> *"Sensemaking is the process that is generative of a special form of meaning, Actionable Intelligence. It toggles between representation of the world & interpretation of its relevance to our presence in it. Convenient though it is to think of, & perform, those functions as separate & somehow sequential, they are really inextricably co-implicated, one happening as the other one is too, encroaching on each other, feeding into each other, blending in a spiralling of significance, vitally necessary & frustratingly impermanent."* [8 Design Principles for generative Sensemaking](https://static1.squarespace.com/static/6358fcee9747a0469c1398a6/t/65856807306a57677fea8579/1703241737324/8+Principles+of+Sensemaking.pdf)

> *"For Weick, interpersonal processes play out as actors know who they are by what they say to others and how others respond to them. He observes, “People verbalize their interpretations and the processes they use to generate them” (Weick 1995: 8). A distinctive feature of sensemaking, and one that also distinguishes it from interpretation, is the way action and organization collaborate to make up the structure. Weick sees communication as a type of action because generating discourse is an act of performance and production. Sensemaking is about “authoring as well as reading” (Weick 1995: 7)."* [The use of narrative to understand and respond to complexity](https://thecynefin.co/wp-content/uploads/2005/01/51-Browning-Boudes-on-Weick-and-Snowden.pdf?srsltid=AfmBOoq5JvyRFhScGslHyMulRDUvmt0EgqKWbNkCDHKCHdosxzmV4FU1)

Interpretation is passive, while sensemaking is active. Interpretation is a one-way process: you observe something and try to understand it, like reading a book and figuring out what it means.

Sensemaking is a process of actively creating meaning through both thinking AND doing, like being both the writer AND reader of a story. It's a two-way process: your actions affect the situation, and the situation affects your understanding.

Interpretation would be like a new employee reading the employee handbook to understand company policies. Sensemaking would be like that same employee:

1. Reading the handbook
2. Trying out the policies in their work
3. Talking to coworkers about how they handle things
4. Adjusting their behavior based on what works
5. Creating new informal practices with colleagues
6. Those new practices eventually becoming "how things are done"

In sensemaking, the structure (how things work) emerges from this back-and-forth between thinking and doing, rather than just existing in a handbook to be interpreted.

## Comparisons
> *"sense-making is about making sense of the external world, while meaning-making is about relating it to our inner world."* [The Difference Between Sense-making and Meaning-making](https://conversational-leadership.net/sensemaking/#:~:text=In%20other%20words%2C%20sense%2Dmaking,it%20to%20our%20inner%20world.)

Sense-making can be understood as:
1. A process-oriented activity:
    - It's described as "verbing" rather than "nouning" (Dervin).
    - It's an ongoing, active process of understanding and interpreting information or situations.
2. A way of structuring the unknown:
    - It involves searching for representations and encoding data to answer specific questions (Russell et al.).
    - It's about making connections among people, places, and events to anticipate their trajectories (Klein et al.).
3. A foundational cognitive activity:
    - It's described as "a mandate of the human condition" (Dervin).
    - It often precedes meaning-making and can occur without necessarily invoking meaning.
4. A cyclical process that can involve:
    - Assimilation of new information
    - Elaboration on existing understanding
    - Questioning and doubting current interpretations
    - Reframing perspectives when new information doesn't fit existing frameworks
5. A key aspect of human-computer interaction:
    - It involves making sense of interfaces, content, and how to interact with digital systems.
    - It can be represented in various ways: spatial, argumentational, faceted, hierarchical, sequential, or networked (Faisal et al.).
6. A process of creating and testing hypotheses:
    - It involves creating "frames" (mental models or hypotheses) and testing them against available data (Klein et al.).

Meaning-making can be understood as:
1. A process of constructing understanding or significance:
    - It's closely associated with the semantics (the meaning) of content.
    - In educational contexts, it's seen as pivotal in knowledge construction.
2. An outcome-oriented activity:
    - It often results in a "naming" or "nouning" - producing a concept or understanding.
    - It's typically describable in terms of nouns and properties, or as propositions.
3. A key component in constructivist learning theory:
    - It's central to how learners build their understanding of the world.
    - It involves interpreting new information in the context of previous knowledge and experiences.
4. A process of creating conceptual relationships:
    - It involves forming concepts and understanding how they relate to each other.
5. An activity that may follow sense-making:
    - While sense-making might involve recognizing patterns or connections, meaning-making involves interpreting what those patterns or connections signify.

Let's illustrate with some examples:
### Complex Dataset
Imagine you're looking at a complex dataset. Sense-making might involve recognizing patterns in the data or understanding how different data points relate to each other. Meaning-making would then involve interpreting what those patterns signify in a broader context - what they tell you about the phenomenon the data represents, or what implications they might have.
### App Exploration
Imagine you're using a new smartphone app. Sense-making would involve exploring the interface, understanding what different buttons do, recognizing patterns in how the app is organized, and forming hypotheses about how to accomplish tasks. You might try different approaches, question your initial assumptions, and gradually build a mental model of how the app works.

After figuring out how to use the app (sense-making), you start to understand why it's useful or important to you (meaning-making). You realize that certain features of the app relate to concepts or activities you're familiar with in the real world. You determine how the app fits into your life or work, and what value it brings. You start to understand how this app relates to other apps or technologies you use. You might realize that the app represents a new trend in technology or changes in how people interact with information.
