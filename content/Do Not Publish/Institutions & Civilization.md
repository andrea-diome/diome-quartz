### Protocols as technology
Protocols are a distinct and crucial type of information technology. At their core, protocols are sets of rules or guidelines that define how entities should interact or behave in specific situations. They are, in essence, the "software" of social systems, providing the structured information that coordinates behavior across multiple components or participants.

Protocols can take many forms, ranging from explicit, codified rules to implicit, unwritten norms. They can be as formal as legal codes or as informal as social etiquette. What unites all protocols is their role in coordinating interactions. For example, the rules of chess are a protocol - they define how players interact and how the game proceeds. In contrast, chess strategies or moves are techniques - methods a player can use to try to win the game.

For a protocol to be effective, it must be adopted and followed by all or most participants in the system. For example, a communication protocol like Morse code only works if both the sender and receiver understand and use the same code.

To understand protocols, let's consider a few more examples:
1. In social interactions, handshake protocols specify how to greet someone in a professional setting. This coordinates behavior between individuals, setting expectations and reducing uncertainty in social encounters. ==(change to marriage or language?)==
2. In computer networking, the TCP/IP protocol suite defines how data should be packaged, addressed, transmitted, and received across the internet. This protocol coordinates the behavior of millions of devices, ensuring they can communicate effectively regardless of their hardware or software specifics.
3. In biology, DNA serves as a protocol for cellular processes, providing instructions for protein synthesis. This coordinates the behavior of cellular components, ensuring that the right proteins are produced at the right time.

Protocols are typically rule-based, specifying what should or shouldn't be done in certain situations. They often have built-in or associated enforcement mechanisms to ensure compliance. Examples of enforcement mechanisms include error checking in computer protocols, which automatically detects and corrects transmission errors; or peer pressure in social etiquette, where breaching protocol may result in social disapproval or exclusion.

In the broader context of our exploration of technology, protocols serve as a critical link between individual components and larger systemic structures. They are the means by which overarching values or goals are translated into coordinated action, and by which materials, energy and information are directed towards specific ends. By providing a shared framework for interaction, protocols enable complex systems to function coherently, whether those systems are digital networks, social institutions, or biological organisms.
### Institutions as networks
Building on our understanding of protocols, we can now explore how institutions function as complex networks of interacting elements. This perspective allows us to see institutions  as dynamic systems that coordinate behavior and shape our world.

Institutions, can be understood through four key elements: 
1. Moral purpose: A set of values that guide their function and goals
2. Protocols: The rules, norms, and practices that define how the institution operates and how its members interact
3. Actants: The people, more-than-human beings, and technological systems that carry out the institution's functions and embody its values
4. Networks: The emergent property created by the interaction of the other three elements, forming a complex web of relationships and interactions

It's important to note that while protocols are sometimes referred to as institutions themselves, our framework views the institution as the synergistic combination of all four elements. This expanded understanding aligns with traditional definitions of institutions as systems that structure behavior, while explicitly acknowledging the role of values and actants, as well as the technological nature of protocols. This perspective is particularly valuable in our increasingly interconnected world, where the boundaries between social institutions and technological systems are increasingly blurred.

Let's explore this concept through several examples:

Internet protocol ecosystems can be viewed as institutions. The TCP/IP protocol suite, for instance, has a moral purpose of enabling global data communication and fostering a connected world. Its protocols are the technical standards defining how data is packaged, transmitted, and received. The actants include computers, routers, programmers implementing the protocols, and billions of users relying on them daily. This network allows for seamless communication across vast distances and diverse hardware.

The institution of marriage illustrates how traditional social institutions can be understood through this framework. Its moral purpose may include values such as love, commitment, and family formation. The protocols include legal contracts, cultural traditions like weddings, and social norms around fidelity. Actants encompass the individuals getting married, their families and communities, and the legal and economic systems supporting the union. This network of actants, bound by shared protocols and purpose, creates the complex social institution we recognize as marriage.

Language provides another clear example. The actants include speakers, listeners, writers, readers, and various communication media. Protocols are the rules of grammar, syntax, and social interaction. The moral purpose is to enable understanding and preserve knowledge. New words and expressions can quickly spread through this network as actants adopt them, demonstrating how institutional networks can evolve over time.

The concept of institutions as networks provides a powerful framework for understanding how institutions coordinate complex behaviors without the need for centralized control. Each actant in the network "knows" how to interact with the others because of the shared protocols. This model also illustrates how institutions adapt: like any network, institutions can absorb local disruptions without overall failure. At the same time, small changes can sometimes propagate rapidly through the network, leading to large-scale shifts.

This network perspective illuminates how institutions interact and overlap. When the institution of language intersects with the internet, for example, we see the emergence of new forms of communication like emojis, memes, and social media shorthand. These innovations arise from the interaction of linguistic protocols with digital platforms, creating new ways for actants to express themselves and interact. Similarly, the institution of marriage interacts with economic institutions (property rights, inheritance laws), political institutions (tax policies, social welfare programs), and cultural institutions (religion, gender norms).

In the broader context of our essay, this network model of institutions forms a crucial link between our understanding of technology as the structuring of materials, energy, and information. ==(Institutions serve as the frameworks through which these elements interact and are directed towards specific purposes. For instance, educational institutions structure information (curricula) and materials (school buildings, textbooks) using energy (student and teacher effort, electricity) to achieve the moral purpose of knowledge dissemination. This interplay of materials, energy, and information through institutional networks provides a framework for understanding how technologies interact within complex social systems.)==

Biological systems illustrate how networks, energy, materials, and information integrate to create life. The human body relies on interconnected networks - cardiovascular for circulating oxygen and nutrients, nervous for processing signals and maintaining homeostasis. Energy from food fuels metabolic processes, while the brain coordinates information flows to regulate systems like respiration and digestion.

Together, society's technological systems and institutions operate as a *civilizational physiology*. Networks like transportation, power grids, and telecommunications form structural pathways akin to circulatory, nervous, and digestive systems. Energy powers economic production, enabling the extraction and transformation of materials. Institutions like markets and governance systems provide regulatory mechanisms to coordinate these networks. 

It's crucial to understand that this networked nature of institutions isn't new or unique to our globalized, high-tech world. Even in smaller-scale societies, these dynamics have always been at play. Language, for instance, has always been a technology connecting people, evolving and adapting to new needs. Families and tribes are social networks that coordinate resources, knowledge, and necessities, forming the building blocks of larger institutional structures.

Some argue that what defines humanity is our use of technology, while others point to our ability to coordinate on a large scale. I would argue it's the synergy of both: the uniqueness of human being stems from our capacity to create and extend these networks of cooperation and influence through technology. From the development of language to the invention of money to the creation of the internet, being human is fundamentally about creating and navigating these ever-expanding institutions, constantly pushing the boundaries of what's possible in terms of coordination through technological achievement.
### Civilization as a meta-institution
> *Civilization  is  an  info-(energetic-)material  structure  developed  by  humans  to effectively cope  with  themselves,  nature,  and  their  perceived  creator.* [A Grand Model of Civilization](https://scholarsarchive.byu.edu/ccr/vol51/iss51/7/)

A civilization can be understood as a complex socio-technological system developed by humans to organize life and meet their collective needs. It's a meta-institution or meta-technology that encompasses and integrates all other technologies, from simple tools to complex social institutions. In essence, **civilization is the most extensive and intricate technology ever constructed by humanity.**

Building on our understanding of institutions as networks, we can view civilization as a network of networks, a vast interconnected system of institutions that collectively shape human existence. This perspective allows us to analyze civilizations through three interconnected layers:

![[CM Civilization.png]]

1. Superstructure: This layer comprises the ideas, beliefs, and values that shape a civilization's worldview. It includes religious and philosophical systems, cultural norms, and collective aspirations. The superstructure provides the moral framework and purpose that guide the development and use of technologies within a civilization.
2. Social Structure: This layer encompasses the organized set of institutions, norms, and relationships that regulate behavior and interactions within a society. It includes political systems, economic structures, educational institutions, and social hierarchies. The social structure determines how technologies are developed, distributed, and utilized within a civilization.
3. Infrastructure: This layer consists of the physical systems and technologies that provision society's essential needs. It includes transportation networks, energy systems, communication technologies, and production facilities. The infrastructure forms the material backbone of a civilization, enabling the flow of resources, energy, and information.

These three layers mirror the institutional framework we discussed earlier, with the superstructure reflecting the moral purpose, the social structure embodying the protocols, and the infrastructure representing the actants. The network that emerges from the interaction of these elements is the civilization itself.

Civilizational systems are deeply influenced by and, in turn, influence biological elements like human physiology, interspecies relationships, and planetary ecosystems. The characteristics of the ecosystem where a civilization develops influences its food system, which will in turn shape its governance and religious beliefs. The opposite is also true: a civilization's worldviews shape the nature of its interactions with humans, other species, and the wider ecosystem, impacting all of them over time.

To illustrate how this framework applies to different types of societies, let's consider two contrasting examples:

1. Indigenous Amazonian Civilization:
    - Superstructure: Animistic beliefs, emphasizing harmony with nature and spiritual connection to the forest.
    - Social Structure: Egalitarian tribal systems, with decisions often made collectively and knowledge passed down orally.
    - Infrastructure: Sustainable forest management techniques, traditional medicine, and hand-crafted tools adapted to the rainforest environment.
2. Ancient Egyptian Civilization:
    - Superstructure: Polytheistic religion, belief in afterlife, and divine right of pharaohs.
    - Social Structure: Hierarchical society with centralized governance, specialized labor roles, and scribal class for record-keeping.
    - Infrastructure: Monumental architecture (pyramids, temples), sophisticated irrigation systems, and advanced embalming techniques.

These three layers of civilization are deeply interconnected and influence each other in complex ways. For instance, the ancient Egyptian belief in the afterlife (superstructure) drove the development of embalming techniques (infrastructure) and supported a priestly class (social structure). Similarly, the Amazonian worldview of harmony with nature (superstructure) shaped sustainable forest management practices (infrastructure) and egalitarian social systems (social structure).

Scholars have debated which of these layers is the primary driver of civilizational development. Materialist theories, like Marxism, argue that the infrastructure (means of production) is the foundation upon which the social structure and superstructure are built. Idealist theories, on the other hand, posit that the superstructure of ideas and beliefs shapes the development of social structures and infrastructure.

In reality, the relationship between these layers is likely more complex and bidirectional. Technological innovations can reshape social structures and belief systems, while new ideas can drive technological development and social reorganization. The agricultural revolution, for instance, led to profound changes in social structure (emergence of hierarchies) and superstructure (new religious systems), while the spread of monotheistic religions reshaped social structures and drove certain technological developments.

Understanding civilization as a meta-institution allows us to appreciate the intricate interplay between technology, social organization, and human beliefs. It highlights how technological change is not merely a matter of material innovation, but a complex process that involves and affects all aspects of human existence.
### Technology and Civilizational Evolution
Our exploration of technology, institutions, and civilization has revealed a complex interplay between materials, energy, information, and human purpose. From simple tools to vast civilizational systems, technology emerges as a fundamental structuring force in human societies.

This perspective raises profound questions about technological and civilizational change. How do innovations in one layer of civilization - such as a new infrastructure technology - ripple through to affect social structures and belief systems? Conversely, how do shifts in worldviews or social organizations drive or constrain technological development? And how do these intricate interactions shape the overall trajectory of human societies?

These questions extend beyond academic interest. In our era of rapid technological advancement and global challenges, understanding the dynamics of technological and civilizational change is crucial. By comprehending these processes, we can gain valuable insights into our past, navigate our present more effectively, and better anticipate and shape possible futures.

### Civilizational transition

Human civilizations have generally stabilized within three broad levels of complex organization, each coevolving with distinct energy sources, materials, information mediums, social structures, and superstructures:

While this advancement is sometimes portrayed as universally positive social progress, such transitions have often come at significant costs and disruptions for the humans involved. For instance, while foraging societies have been described as "the original affluent society," the shift to agrarian lifestyles frequently entailed more arduous and monotonous labor. Similarly, the industrialization process violently displaced countless individuals from their homes and livelihoods through enclosures and forced urbanization.

1. **Foraging Societies:**
    - **Energy Source:** Hunting and gathering.
    - **Materials:** Natural resources directly available from the environment.
    - **Information Medium:** Ritualized Language.
    - **Societal Structure:** Small, egalitarian bands and tribes.
    - **Superstructure:** Animism and nature-based spiritual beliefs. Societies viewed themselves as part of the natural world, with a deep respect for the environment and its resources.
2. **Agrarian Societies:**
    - **Energy Source:** Agriculture (domesticated plants and animals).
    - **Materials:** Cultivated land, domesticated animals.
    - **Information Medium:** Writing.
    - **Societal Structure:** Chiefdoms and kingdoms with hierarchical structures.
    - **Superstructure:** Poly- and monotheism upholding the divine right of rulers. Belief systems often centered around agricultural deities and the sanctity of the king or chieftain as a mediator between the gods and the people.
3. **Industrial Societies:**
    - **Energy Source:** Fossil fuels (coal, oil, natural gas).
    - **Materials:** Industrial raw materials (metals, minerals).
    - **Information Medium:** Printing press and telecommunications.
    - **Societal Structure:** Nation-states with centralized governance.
    - **Superstructure:** Rationalism and nationalism. Enlightenment ideals emphasized scientific progress, individual rights, and national identity. Growth became a dominant ideology.

It's important to note that more research is still needed to fully understand the nuanced dynamics driving these civilizational upheavals. For example, one theory posits that the pivotal transition from foraging to agrarian societies was primarily catalyzed by the extraction of wealth through conquest and subjugation (financial extraction), rather than being solely propelled by the invention of writing systems. A more holistic understanding of these processes is crucial as we navigate the pressures and opportunities of our current civilizational crossroads.
### Civilizational Failure
Civilizations have collapsed catastrophically many times throughout history, and there are recurring patterns that have contributed to their demise. These underlying drivers of civilizational failure are now intensifying, posing unprecedented risks in our modern era.
